SLIDE 6 - "Prompt and pray vs. spec first"
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ON SCREEN
  Two columns. Prompt and pray: decisions in chat scrollback, re-explaining the app,
  "done" means the AI says so, ripples nobody sees, history is memory.
  Spec Kit: decisions written before code, a constitution, multiple-choice questions,
  numbered tasks, converge checks code vs. spec, all in git.

SAY  [~2 min]
  So how did that get built? Let's compare two ways of working with an AI.

  On the left is what I'll call "prompt and pray." Your decisions live in chat
  scrollback. Every prompt, you re-explain the app. "Done" means the AI says it's done.
  A change ripples into places nobody looks. And your history is whatever you remember.

  On the right is spec-driven development. The spec is the source of truth, and the code
  is its output. You write the what and the why first, with no tech. Then the how. Then
  an ordered task list. Only then do you build, and afterwards you check what got built
  against the spec.

  Your job as the human moves from typing code to making decisions and reviewing text.

  And decisions before code are cheap. Example: while reviewing the data model, I saw the
  plan was to store money as integer cents. So a $200.18 purchase would be stored as
  20018. I said, "I really don't like that." Changing it then cost one sentence. After
  five hundred tasks, it would have been a rewrite.

  Same review, I moved product data out of JSON files into SQLite, and I asked whether
  anything in the plan would need expensive hosting like Vercel.

  And the last line here, "converge," is a big one. After the AI said a feature was done,
  converge checked the code against the spec. It found real gaps in nine of twelve
  features.

  [CLICK]

IF ASKED

  catalog data in code; nosy fields never stored; an LLM never invents a price.

SLIDE 17 - "The commands, in order"
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ON SCREEN
  1 /constitution  2 /specify  3 /clarify  4 /plan  5 /tasks  6 /analyze
  7 /implement  8 /converge

SAY  [~2.5 min]
  These are the slash commands you run in your AI agent, in order. Each one is
  /speckit-something.

  One, constitution. The project's laws. It's versioned with semantic versioning.
  Mine was created at 1.0.0 and amended seven times.

  Two, specify. Takes your idea and turns it into stories, requirements and success
  criteria. No tech. It's allowed at most three "needs clarification" markers. For
  everything else, it makes an informed default and writes it down as an assumption.

  Three, clarify. This is my favorite. It asks you up to five questions, one at a time,
  multiple choice, and it gives you its recommendation. Then it writes your answer into
  the spec. My answers looked like: "Q1, C, and I think the countdown showing
  milliseconds would be extra intense. Q2, C. Q3, B."

  Four, plan. Stack, data model, contracts. It has to pass the constitution check. For
  the first feature, it picked Next.js, SQLite, Drizzle, Vitest and Playwright. I
  reviewed it file by file in VS Code.

  Five, tasks. Numbered tasks with file paths, tests before code. Between 14 and 83 per
  feature.

  Six, analyze. A read-only cross-check of the spec, plan and tasks. Findings get IDs,
  and anything that conflicts with the constitution is automatically critical. Nothing
  changes until you approve.

  Seven, implement. It builds task by task and checks the boxes. You can scope it, like
  "T001 through T040."

  Eight, converge. Compares the code to the spec and adds a task for every gap. You run
  it again until it says "Converged."

  [CLICK]

IF ASKED
  - Analyze ID letters: A = ambiguity, C = coverage, D = duplication,
    I = inconsistency, U = underspecified.
  - Also available: /speckit-checklist ("unit tests for English") and
    /speckit-taskstoissues.

SLIDE 18 - "The constitution"
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ON SCREEN
  Eight principles. Two example rules: money is exact two-decimal USD strings via
  big.js; a bot is a voice, not a source.

SAY  [~2 min]
  You saw the file a few minutes ago. Here's what's in it. The constitution is where the
  non-negotiables live. It's what kept the AI from drifting over two weeks and twelve
  features.

  It holds two kinds of rules. Comedy rules, like "The store must never explain the
  joke," and "Every feature must answer one question: does this make him buy another
  shirt?"

  And engineering rules. Money is exact two-decimal strings, never JavaScript number
  math. Product data lives only in the database. The full verify script has to pass
  before any merge. The nosy sign-up fields are never stored. Card numbers are never
  stored.

  It gets checked at plan time, again at analyze time, where a violation is always
  critical, and again at converge.

  My favorite moment of the whole project happened here. Claude's first draft of
  principle four was "Aggressively Upbeat, Never Deceptive." No fake scarcity, no fake
  timers, no pre-checked boxes. Which is the responsible default! But the whole joke is
  that Dan Flashes is manipulative. So I pushed back, and it got ratified as "Shamelessly
  Manipulative."

  That one decision turned into two entire features: the Urgency Engine and the Checkout
  Squeeze.

SAY  [~2 min]

  1.0.0 was that "Shamelessly Manipulative" change.
  2.0.0: the draft said "You set scores, never prices." I said, not true. I set the final

  A lesson I learned about semver: I had four major version bumps in the first two days.
  At one point I literally asked, "what are the minor versions even for?" After that, I
  batched amendments. One draft per feature, the version bump justified, and ratified
  when that feature's PR merges.

  And you don't need anyone's initials to ratify it. Git records who changed the line
  and when.

SLIDE 31 - "Why it burned so fast"  (The cons, part 2)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ON SCREEN
  Every turn re-sends everything / The 1M window hid the cost / Features chained in one
  session / Long unattended runs / Noisy output in the main thread / Opus, high effort,
  for everything.

SAY  [~1.5 min]
  Why did it burn so fast?

  Every model call re-sends the whole conversation so far. So at half a million tokens a
  turn, even a two-word message like "merge away" cost more than an entire long chat in a
  fresh session.

  The huge context window actually hid the cost. Compaction only kicked in near a million
  tokens, so most of the work ran with an enormous context.

  I chained features in one session. On my busiest day, I shipped three features and
  started a fourth, all in the same conversation. 643 calls in one day.

  I had long unattended runs, like that 4 AM "implement your recommendation for all."

  All the noisy output went into the main thread. Ten-minute test runs, retries,
  performance reports. Almost twenty-seven hundred shell commands.

  And I used the biggest model at high effort for everything, including re-running tests
  and fixing formatting. At one point I even asked, "should I bump the effort level?"
  It went up, not down.

  The one thing I did right: batching. Most of my feedback came as one message with every
  answer and every bug in it, instead of a stream of little messages.

SLIDE 32 - "Start fresh. Specs remember."  (The fix)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ON SCREEN
  New session per feature / /clear between features / Watch the context / Match the
  model to the phase / Scope implement runs / Subagents for noisy work.
  "Rough estimate: an average context of ~150K instead of ~500K reads about a third of
  the tokens."

SAY  [~2 min]
  So here's what I'd do differently. And the irony is that Spec Kit is built for exactly
  this. I just used it like one long pair-programming call.

  Start a new session per feature, or even per phase. Everything a fresh session needs is
  already on disk: the constitution, the spec, the plan, the task list with its
  checkboxes, the roadmap. A new chat can start with "implement, continue 012 from the
  first unchecked task." When I actually did run out of tokens mid-feature, picking back
  up was painless for exactly that reason.

  Use /clear between features. Only compact in the middle of one.

  Watch the context. Wrap up a phase around 150 to 200 thousand tokens, not a million.

  Match the model to the phase. Use the big model for specify, plan and analyze, where
  judgment matters. Use a smaller model for implement and test loops, which are more
  mechanical.

  Scope your implement runs. "T001 through T012, then stop." Not "all of it" overnight.

  And use subagents for noisy work. They run the tests and read the logs in their own
  context, and only the verdict comes back.

  Rough estimate: averaging 150K of context instead of 500K reads about a third of the
  tokens.

Q: How long did it actually take?
A: Twelve calendar days, Oct 12-23, with commits on ten of them. [Add your own sense of
   how many hours a day you put in.]

Q: Would you use Spec Kit on a client project?
A: For features, yes: the up-front questions and the converge step are worth it. For
   small fixes, no; the ceremony is too much. And I'd run a fresh session per phase.

Q: Why not just write a good prompt?
A: The prompt disappears into scrollback. The spec is a file in git that every future
   session, and every teammate, can read. And clarify asks the questions I wouldn't have
   thought to answer.

Q: Did the AI ever ignore the constitution?
A: Analyze treats a constitution conflict as CRITICAL, and converge re-checks after
   implementation. Converge found gaps in 9 of 12 features after the AI said they were
   done, which is exactly why that step exists.

Q: Which AI did you use?
A: Claude Code, on Opus, in VS Code. (In hindsight: Opus for the judgment phases, a
   smaller model for implement.)

Q: How much did it cost?
A: It ran on a subscription, so the cost was my weekly allowance, and I ran out two days
   early. About 1.47 billion cached tokens read across 2,915 calls. With a fresh session
   per feature, I'd estimate about a third of that.

Q: Why a local model for the chatbot instead of an API?
A: No cost, no keys, nothing leaves the machine, and it was a good test of "a bot is a
   voice, not a source": even a small, sometimes-wrong model is safe when the store
   controls every price, link and button.

Q: Is the bot safe from prompt injection?
A: It has no tools, no database and no writes. Someone can fake earlier chat turns since
   history comes from the browser, but the worst they get is off-script wording.
